# Typography Bulletin: June 2026

### This month's bulletin features notable developments discussing artificial intelligence, font ownership, independent type foundries, archival projects on vernacular lettering, and the cultural authority of typography.

⏰ **Reading Time:** ~10 minutes

Hello everyone!

The June bulletin opens with font selection moving to chat interfaces. While receiving recommendations without browsing a catalog may seem like a practical convenience, it also raises the question of what commercial logic drives font discovery. Along the same lines, the economics of type is being debated anew through intellectual property transfers, new corporate structures, and independent sales infrastructures. On the street level, while the Los Angeles and Amsterdam archives remind us how signage carries urban memory, the transition of the Ankara typeface onto a football jersey demonstrates public identity being carried over to another surface. Meanwhile, AI companies turning to serif fonts and visual aesthetics being packaged under algorithmic labels make visible how letterforms are used today to generate trust, cultural authority, and brand language.

## Platforms, Ownership, and the Economics of Type

*Image: [Monotype](https://www.monotype.com/company/press-release/monotype-launches-myfonts-app-chatgpt-bringing-real-font-discovery-ai)*

Monotype has brought the MyFonts app to the ChatGPT platform, [embedding](https://www.monotype.com/company/press-release/monotype-launches-myfonts-app-chatgpt-bringing-real-font-discovery-ai) font discovery directly into the chat interface. Currently launched in the US market, the app allows users to receive font recommendations in English conversations by using the *@MyFonts* command. It is worth noting that turning the catalog search experience into a recommendation engine obscures the editorial layer of font selection, as which target audience, commercial priority, or dataset shapes the recommendation remains outside the interface.

TypeToday's interview with Sam Berlow [looks](https://type.today/en/journal/thetypefounders) at the type industry from a different angle. Proposing a model that acquires designers' intellectual property and assumes commercial and marketing operations, The Type Founders allows designers to focus solely on designing type. Featuring designers such as Mark Simonson and Matthew Carter, the model positions itself against Monotype on the premise that selling the foundry is not the only way out. While easing designers' operational burden is advantageous, transferring intellectual property may not be easily embraced by type designers. Consequently, designers' willingness to participate will determine the sustainability of the model.

A bill debated in the Colorado Senate puts forward a more technical proposal on how creative property can be protected within a corporate structure. The [Artist Corporation](https://www.artistcorporations.com/), or A-Corp model, mandates that at least 51 percent of voting shares remain with the artist. In the event of dissolution, returning intellectual property to the creator rather than transferring it to investors could provide robust protection for enduring assets such as type families. This framework relies on contractual architecture rather than a romantic promise of independence. The goal is to explicitly define which party retains decision-making authority while capital is being provided.

The fact that [Fontdue](https://www.fontdue.com/) has served more than 100 type foundries since 2018 is another data point reminding us that independent font sales are still seeking their own infrastructure. The platform enables type foundries to manage sales, licensing, and checkout flows through their own domain names. Dinamo consolidating its publications onto a single website can likewise be [read](https://editions.abcdinamo.com/) in a similar light. Type foundries no longer merely distribute font files; they are also establishing their own publishing and information architectures.

## Labels, Artificial Intelligence, and Visual Authority

*Image: [De Stijl, 1922](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/elizabeth-goodspeed-whats-in-a-name-categorising-aesthetics-creative-industry-190526)*

Elizabeth Goodspeed [examines](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/elizabeth-goodspeed-whats-in-a-name-categorising-aesthetics-creative-industry-190526) the growing prevalence of the question "What aesthetic is this?" through the lens of algorithmic classification culture. Grouping images under the same label based on visual similarities, divorced from their historical, social, and material contexts, is not an innocent classification practice. Goodspeed emphasizes that naming has become an act of establishing cultural authority rather than merely being descriptive, and that whoever first coins a label can rise to the position of a cultural commentator without having produced anything. Trend-forecasting companies, in turn, directly convert this informal cultural capital into a commercial product.

AI companies turning to serif typefaces represents the more corporate side of this same representational economy. The preference of brands like Claude and Manus for serif typefaces is [associated](https://www.wired.com/story/ai-has-come-for-serif-fonts/) by critics with the concept of *tasteslop*, which signifies an effort to project a facade of artificial refinement. According to San Francisco-based designer Keya Vadgama, this choice is a strategy to signal humanity in order to gloss over artificial intelligence's inherently cold and impersonal nature; after all, the calligraphically rooted essence of serif typefaces makes them more fluid and human. When prompted to explain its own design choices, Claude explains this trend through the pursuit of trust, authority, and literary gravitas. The choice of letterforms here is not merely a visual decision; it is a declaration of which cultural memory the tech company wishes to align itself with. That is why Vadgama's remark resonates so well: "Even if you use Comic Sans, you’re still an AI company." Typefaces can soften the chill, but they cannot change what you are.

## Archives, Street Lettering, and the Material History of the Letter

> "Signs are actors. Some are wallflowers, barely speaking above a whisper, while others shout and flirt, beckoning for attention."
>
> — E. Goodspeed

Goodspeed [re-examines](https://www.sunsetoversunset.org/stories/signs) Ed Ruscha's photographic archive of Sunset Boulevard in Los Angeles, spanning from 1966 to 2007, from a typographic perspective. Ranging from neon signs and hand-painted lettering to psychedelic 1970s letterforms and corporate brand identities, this visual history demonstrates through concrete examples how each era inscribed its cultural priorities and production technologies into the form of letters.

Arno Verweij's [Amsterdam Typography](https://www.amsterdamtypography.nl/) and [Netherlands Typography](https://www.netherlandstypography.nl/) archives [document](https://bl.ag/amsterdam-lettering-and-typography-through-the-ages/) the same domain in a more systematic manner. Having accumulated over 4,000 photographs across the two sites since 2019, the archive—together with a special 1,850-image section curated from the City Archives' digital image bank for Amsterdam's 750th anniversary—extends its earliest examples back to 1857. This visual continuity, reaching from an 1877 painted wall advertisement to the curly calligraphic letters (*krulletters*) of 1958, shows that vernacular typography can only be fully comprehended through a sustained and patient archival practice.

While sign painting is often described as a lost or nostalgic trade, Nan Parati hand-lettering more than 3,000 signs each year in New Orleans [stands as a rebuttal](https://www.printmag.com/type-tuesday/legendary-jazz-fest-sign-writer-nan-parati-makes-music-with-her-lettering/?mc_cid=915924123f&mc_eid=48a95a3cf1) to this assumption. Having prepared the signs for Jazz Fest for 42 years, Parati almost single-handedly carries the memory of the festival's typography through her personal styles named *Fun Times Roman* and *Nanuscript*. The fact that young sign painters are recirculating this craft through workshops, communities like [Burds of the Brush](https://medium.com/tipografi/burds-of-the-brush-dayan%C4%B1%C5%9Fmayla-b%C3%BCy%C3%BCyen-bir-topluluk-a206f7cce6d5), and independent work shows that street lettering continues to thrive not only in archives, but also on the workbench.

News of Gençlerbirliği from Turkey offers an apt example of the intersection between urban identity and sports. According to information shared by [Yaşar Soylu](https://x.com/yasarsoyluuu/status/2067261648532840822?s=20) from TRT Spor, the club will use Oğuzhan Cengiz's Ankara typeface on its new-season jerseys. The typeface won first prize in the font category of a competition launched by the Ankara Metropolitan Municipality in 2021. Thus, a typeface designed for public signage gains visibility on the football jersey of the very same city. This development operates differently from custom-commissioned typefaces: here, the city embraces the typeface first, and the team subsequently adopts it. We previously shared these developments with our readers in our [May](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-haberleri-mayıs-2021-d8cf2966d3ea) and [August](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-haberleri-a%C4%9Fustos-2021-b87f55e89171) 2021 bulletins. The examples examined in our article titled "[Letters on the Pitch](https://medium.com/tipografi/sahadaki-harfler-spor-markalamas%C4%B1nda-tipografinin-y%C3%BCkselen-g%C3%BCc%C3%BC-8f249d1300ad)" analyzed custom typefaces commissioned for clubs. The relationship urban typography establishes with brand identity was also opened for discussion at the panel "[From City to Brand: Sakarya](https://www.satso.org.tr/komitehaber/20621/satso-12--meslek-komitesi-ve-ared-is-birligindeki-etkinlik-sektor-paydaslarini-bir-araya-getirdi.aspx)" held on June 16, 2026. During the panel, one of our editors, Murat Ertürk, discussed how signage and vernacular lettering styles shaped Sakarya's visual identity through his book "[Letters of Sakarya](https://www.muraterturk.com/book.html)". This example serves as a reminder that vernacular typography is not merely archival material belonging to the past, but an active layer still operating in how cities define, remember, and brand themselves.

There is also significant activity on the archival desk. Eve Scarborough of Letterform Archive [reports](https://letterformarchive.org/news/from-the-collection-emigre-font-development-files/) that the digitization of Emigre's font development files has made the early digital typography production processes of the 1980s and 1990s far more legible. Proof prints, handwritten corrections, correspondence, and reference materials document a design culture where analog and digital production were not divorced from one another, but rather intertwined on the very same workspace.

A video investigating the origins of Lorem Ipsum [distances itself](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kL1PDqzqhM4) from the widespread assumption that the text has been handed down unchanged since the 1500s. It explains that the form in use today was standardized especially during the 20th century within Letraset and modern typesetting culture, while its roots trace back to Cicero's classical Latin texts. Jeff Jarvis's forthcoming book "[Hot Type](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9798765123959)", to be published in the coming months by Bloomsbury Academic, similarly addresses typesetting technology alongside media history, examining how the Linotype machine transformed the newspaper, book, and magazine industries through the lens of the birth of mass communication.

At Commercial Type, Paul Barnes's Action Grotesque [article](https://commercialtype.com/about/collections/action_grotesque) recalls Walter Tracy's *Adsans* and *Doric* typefaces designed for Linotype alongside Harry Carter's principles of optical adjustment. The way Van Blokland's function-first approach for text sizes transforms into an unexpected character at display sizes effectively demonstrates that optical scaling is far more than dry mathematics.

## Order to return to Times New Roman

*Image: © [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2025/dec/10/trump-times-new-roman-font-return-state-department)*

The US Department of State [announced](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2025/dec/10/trump-times-new-roman-font-return-state-department) in a memo dated December 9, 2025, that it is returning to Times New Roman for official correspondence. The Department claimed that Calibri—which had been chosen during the Biden era on the grounds of offering higher readability for individuals with dyslexia and visual impairments—undermined institutional seriousness. Marco Rubio, who proposed the change, stated that abandoning Calibri would eliminate yet another wasteful DEIA (inclusive practices initiated across many areas during the Biden era) program.

A response was not long in coming from Lucas de Groot, who designed Calibri for Microsoft in 2004. Countering the *waste* argument with an unemotional [statement](https://www.instagram.com/p/DSIRAvGDNdc/) grounded entirely in legibility, screen technology, and technical shortcomings, de Groot emphasized that Times New Roman was designed not for digital screens, but for newspaper printing where ink bleeds into paper, and that it creates visual noise on modern screens, hindering readability. Drawing attention to technical carelessness in the digital version—particularly inconsistent spacing in uppercase letters that undermines a professional appearance—the designer stated that this decision takes the administration not only into the past, but also in a direction that is technically and functionally inferior.

## Seoul’s transit network transitions to a new type system after forty years.

*Image: © [Print](https://www.printmag.com/type-tuesday/latin-hangul-visual-harmony-seoul-metro-type-system/)*

The first major visual communication and wayfinding overhaul in forty years is beginning across Seoul’s public transit system. The new type system to be used across the entire network, from subways to buses, is based on the [Seoul Arim](https://www.printmag.com/type-tuesday/latin-hangul-visual-harmony-seoul-metro-type-system/) type family, developed through a collaborative effort between Dutch July Type and Korea-based Tlab. Uniting Latin and Hangul characters under a single cohesive structure, Seoul Arim offers both a calm and humane tone through its balanced proportions, open counters, and low contrast that enhance legibility. Seoul Arim will begin to be used throughout the entire transit network in 2026.

## Fran Sans: The typeface keeping San Francisco’s disappearing typography alive

*Image: © [Emily Sneddon](https://emilysneddon.com/fran-sans-essay)*

Developed by Emily Sneddon, [Fran Sans](https://emilysneddon.com/fran-sans-essay) is a new typeface inspired by the LCD destination panels of San Francisco’s light rail system. Utilizing the fixed segments and small grid layout of the boards, the typeface demonstrates to the designer how constraints shape the creative process, while also helping preserve a disappearing typographic heritage. Comprising three members—*Solid, Tile*, and *Panel*—Sneddon aims with Fran Sans to revive the analog and slightly gritty typographic aesthetic in the digital realm, as the original boards will be replaced with LED screens and this visual language will be lost.

## Generational typographic attitudes in Monotype’s report

*Image: © [Monotype](https://www.monotype.com/generational-attitudes-to-type-survey)*

The report titled “[Fonts, Feels & Reels: Generational Attitudes Toward Typography](https://www.monotype.com/generational-attitudes-to-type-survey),” published by Monotype in collaboration with Censuswide, reveals that typography directly influences user perception in digital communication. According to the research conducted with 12,000 participants from six countries, 69% of respondents consider typeface selection important when creating content. Even more striking is that 78% of participants stated that distinctive and well-chosen typefaces increase engagement on social media. Additionally, 65% of participants believe that the right typeface ensures message clarity and comprehensibility. On the brand perception side, 52% of Millennials state that typeface choice directly shapes their trust and loyalty toward a brand.

Examining these quantitative data presented by the report within the context of design practice and user psychology, Charles Nix [interpreted](https://www.fastcompany.com/91445670/a-new-generation-requires-font-sophistication-and-more) the findings through Gen Z’s forms of digital expression. Nix notes that Gen Z and Millennials position typography not merely as a tool for conveying text, but as a primary form of self-expression replacing handwriting. While handwriting usage declines with digitalization, digital typography filling this void transforms into a vehicle reflecting emotional tone for younger generations. However, the limited font options offered by current social media platforms fall short of meeting this need for *authenticity*. According to the report, 76% of Gen Z turn to off-platform design tools when creating content due to these constraints. The article emphasizes that brands should not overlook this typographic awareness, as the right typographic choice is a strategic element that directly increases engagement.

## Type Network 2025 report

*Image: © [Type Network](https://typenetwork.com/articles/looking-ahead-to-2026)*

In its evaluation published as 2026 approaches, Type Network [announced](https://typenetwork.com/articles/looking-ahead-to-2026) that 13 new type foundries joined the platform and a total of 351 new type families were added to the catalog throughout 2025. While Sweet Sans Pro became the most licensed type family of the year, overall licensing data shows that sans serif type families have once again claimed the top spot. Type Network associates this picture with strengthening expectations for functionality, flexibility, and long-term use in typefaces.

## Adoption of variable fonts falls behind expectations!

*Image: © [Type Network](https://typenetwork.com/articles/enthusiasm-for-variable-fonts-outpace-adoption)*

Although there is great interest in variable fonts, which provide both flexibility and efficiency by bringing all the diversity of a type family—such as weight, width, and style—into a single file, actual adoption rates in projects remain well behind expectations. [Type Network data](https://typenetwork.com/articles/enthusiasm-for-variable-fonts-outpace-adoption) shows that the share of variable fonts in paid licensing remains below even 1%. The primary reason for this situation lies not in technical shortcomings, but in the economic barriers of licensing models. While designers generally prefer to purchase the individual static styles required for a project, the high pricing of variable fonts covering the entire family negatively affects purchasing decisions. Indeed, in subscription systems where additional costs are eliminated (such as Adobe Fonts, etc.), usage is seen to increase proportionally with library availability. Therefore, the real obstacle standing in the way of structural advantages such as flexible interface design, performance, and accessibility is not a designer adaptation problem, but market dynamics…

## Interaction in fluid typography

*Image: © [electricmagicfactory.com](https://electricmagicfactory.com/articles/interactive-fluid-typography/)*

Jesús Olano [explores](https://electricmagicfactory.com/articles/interactive-fluid-typography/) the interpolation capabilities offered by variable font technology within an interactive context. Fonts becoming responsive not only to screen size, but also to user behaviors such as cursor movement, scroll speed, or device orientation, transforms typography from a static reading experience into a living interface component. Olano discusses with examples how this fluidity can evolve into an expressive narrative tool in design.

## History of the invisible mark: From Capitulum to Pilcrow

*Image: © [H&Co](https://www.typography.com/blog/pilcrow-capitulum)*

Authoring an essay on the paragraph mark (pilcrow, ¶), Jonathan Hoefler [touches upon](https://www.typography.com/blog/pilcrow-capitulum) how this mark—whose origins trace back to the Latin *Capitulum*, meaning *chapter*—reached its contemporary form through visual metamorphosis over the centuries.

The text reveals that the mark actually has nothing to do with the P in the word paragraph; its original shape was a letter *C*, and the vertical strokes added to it extended downward over time, creating the illusion of a *reverse P* during the Renaissance. Serving as a powerful example of the lasting effect of scribal practice on the shapes of glyphs, this mark—usually *invisible* in modern applications—continues to remain a functional challenge for type designers.

## The debate over copyright protection for fonts is back on the agenda

*Image: © [Matthew Butterick](https://matthewbutterick.com/chron/the-copyrightability-of-fonts-revisited.html)*

Matthew Butterick [argues](https://matthewbutterick.com/chron/the-copyrightability-of-fonts-revisited.html) that the copyright strategies the type industry has long relied upon may not be as legally sound as presumed. Following the *Laatz v. Zazzle* lawsuit, Butterick brings up the reality that fonts are not protected by copyright as visual design under US law. Reminding readers that the industry has attempted to overcome this loophole by classifying and registering fonts as computer software, Butterick emphasizes that this approach does not provide absolute assurance in the eyes of the courts. In his view, in the current environment of legal uncertainty, the most rational strategy for independent designers is not filing lawsuits, but establishing quality market relationships…

## Designing Gotham

*Image: © [Frere-Jones](https://frerejones.com/blog/designing-gotham)*

Following the sale of Hoefler & Co. to Monotype in 2021, and with designs like Gotham *now appearing detached from their origins*, Doug Wilson penned a comprehensive [article](https://frerejones.com/blog/designing-gotham). The article conveys from a technical and historical perspective how Gotham, which began in 2000 as a search for identity for GQ magazine, was derived from local New York signage. A great read to learn the true story of Gotham.

## On balance and progression in type design

*Image: © [Typofonderie](https://typofonderie.com/gazette/looking-for-balance-and-progression)*

Typofonderie published Evert Bloemsma’s 2000 [article](https://typofonderie.com/gazette/looking-for-balance-and-progression) questioning the search for balance and progression in type design. Bloemsma discusses in technical detail how the typographic color of letterforms can be consistently maintained across the different weights of a type family, and the importance of aesthetic rhythm perceived by the eye beyond mechanical construction.

Dutch graphic designer and type designer Evert Bloemsma (1958–2005) studied at the Arnhem Academy of Art. Focusing on functionality in his type designs and pursuing forms suited to the needs of the era, the designer is renowned for his typefaces *FF Balance*, *FF Cocon*, *FF Avance*, and *FF Legato*.

## American Sign Museum’s gold leaf collection

*Image: © [BLAG](https://bl.ag/glorious-gilding-samples-at-the-american-sign-museum/)*

> “Selling a gold leaf job is much easier when you can show your client what can be done, rather than just telling them.”

Published on the occasion of the American Sign Museum’s 25th anniversary, the book brings to light gold leaf samples of critical importance to typography history and sign painting. Dating from the late 19th to early 20th century, these rare pieces document the relationship letter anatomy establishes with reverse glass techniques and light, through benchmark works crafted by masters such as Raymond J. LeBlanc and George C. Brooke. With this publication, the museum turns the artisans’ strategy of providing visual evidence over verbal explanation, along with nearly forgotten material knowledge, into a resource for contemporary type designers.

## Systematic and conceptual transformation of letterforms: “Alphabetical Playground”

*Image: © [Printmag](https://www.printmag.com/typography/letterforms-are-containers-for-ideas-in-alphabetical-playground/)*

Pentagram art director Nigel Cottier’s book “[Alphabetical Playground](https://www.printmag.com/typography/letterforms-are-containers-for-ideas-in-alphabetical-playground/)” approaches the alphabet not merely as fixed counterparts of phonetic sounds, but as *containers* for graphic systems and conceptual ideas. It serves as a fine example of abstracting letterforms from their traditional burdens of meaning and legibility requirements, transforming typography into a playground where pattern, texture, motion, and formal diversity are explored.

## Farewell to a titan of calligraphy: “The Master of Meşk”

*Image: © “[*The Master of Meşk: An Extended Interview with Hasan Çelebi*](https://www.albarakakultur.com/meskin-efendisi)”*

Focusing on the life and artistic journey of Hasan Çelebi (1937–2025), who passed away this past February, the book titled “[The Master of Meşk: An Extended Interview with Hasan Çelebi](https://www.albarakakultur.com/meskin-efendisi)” was published under the byline of Feride Bozcu. Taking the reader on a journey starting from Çelebi’s childhood into Istanbul’s madrasa atmosphere, meşk circles, and the deep master-apprentice relationship he formed with masters from the Ottoman legacy, the book leads toward modern workshops where hundreds of students were trained.

## Winners of the TDC scholarships announced.

*Image: © [TDC](https://tdc.org/news/2025-ade-hogue-beatrice-warde-and-tdc-scholarships-announced/)*

The Type Directors Club, the world’s leading typography organization, [announced](https://tdc.org/news/2025-ade-hogue-beatrice-warde-and-tdc-scholarships-announced/) this year’s winners of its student scholarship programs. The Adé Hogue Scholarship, sponsored by Monotype in memory of designer Adé Hogue, who passed away at a young age in 2021, was shared between Katherine Ispache from the California Institute of the Arts and Charitssa Stone from the Yale School of Art.

The winner of the scholarship awarded in memory of Beatrice Warde, a key figure in typographic history and the TDC’s first female member, was Siyun Li from the School of Visual Arts. In addition, general merit scholarships were awarded to undergraduate students studying at institutions such as Rhode Island School of Design, ArtCenter College of Design, Parsons, and Bauhaus-University Weimar who demonstrated outstanding talent in the fields of typographic form, composition, and design criticism.

## Google Sans and Google Sans are now open source

*Image: © [Google](https://design.google/library/google-sans-flex-font)*

Google is [making](https://design.google/library/google-sans-flex-font) its iconic type family [Google Sans](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Google+Sans) and its variable font version [Google Sans Flex](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Google+Sans+Flex)—which allows designers to make adjustments along six different axes—open source. This move means that the type family can now be used freely and at no cost across the entire digital world, not just in Google products. Born out of necessity following the logo change in 2015 and evolving over time to solve many challenges from small-size legibility to coding, this family aims to create a more consistent and accessible web experience.

## New typeface from Element Type: Zerk

*Image: © [Element Type](https://www.instagram.com/p/DSVFPPjCPK0/?img_index=1)*

Founded by İbrahim Kaçtıoğlu and Doğukan Karapınar, [Element Type](https://elementtype.co/) announced [Zerk](https://www.instagram.com/p/DSVFPPjCPK0/?img_index=1), a typeface featuring retro and futuristic elements. Built upon calligraphic roots, the typeface combines this foundation with exaggerated letter widths and a pre-millennium aesthetic. Featuring eight weights in its initial release, Zerk’s structure thrives on the contrast between sculptural curves and constructed geometry. The type foundry describes Zerk not as *ambitious progress*, but as a vision of the future built on optimism, joy, and playfulness.

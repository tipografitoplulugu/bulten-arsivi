# Typography Newsletter: September 2026

### This month’s bulletin features selections from the world of typography, ranging from Monotype’s library reaching 400,000 styles to the grade axis in variable fonts, and from the Tschichold archive to custom typefaces.

⏰ **Reading Time:** ~10 minutes

Hello everyone!

This month’s developments highlight how much of a letter originates from a single center versus how much comes from the designer’s hand and the archives. While typeface libraries reach hundreds of thousands of styles, brands’ typefaces are being integrated into AI-powered design tools. All of this calls for a reconsideration of the designer’s role within this new order. As new archives are unveiled, identity projects seek letters in the hand gestures of sign language. First, we examine the room left for the designer by scale and AI, followed by what archives and materials offer, and finally where brands source letters for their identities. Exhibition, festival, book, and educational announcements on the calendar can be found in Small Points.

## Scale, AI, and Variable Fonts

*Image: [Advertisements prepared for Erbar Light, Bold Condensed, and Medium Condensed from a late 1930s type specimen](https://cjtype.com/dunbar-design-repository/articles/on-condensed-geometric-sans-serifs-by-florian-hardwig/) © Florian Hardwig*

Monotype [announced](https://www.monotype.com/company/press-release/monotype-library-reaches-400000-font-styles-expanding-choice-across-languages) that its library has reached over 400,000 typeface styles and more than 300 languages. While the company attributes this growth to contributions from more than 5,000 type foundries and an over 75 percent increase in the number of typefaces supporting non-Latin writing systems in 2026, it links the expansion to the goal of helping brands speak authentically in local markets while maintaining their global identities. Meanwhile, the company’s [partnership](https://www.monotype.com/company/press-release/monotype-kittl-expand-partnership-bring-brand-typography-to-creative-workflows) with Kittl, launched in 2024, has expanded to encompass enterprise workflows. Monotype customers can now directly access their organizations’ brand typeface libraries from within Kittl’s AI-powered design environment—an access also leveraged beyond designers by marketing teams, agencies, and regional offices. The two companies aim to make it easier for distributed teams to use approved typography and to strengthen brand governance by centralizing typeface licensing and version management.

Distributing an approved typeface to everyone is not enough to deliver the same appearance to everyone. You know that paper, ink, screens, and backgrounds cause the same typeface to be perceived differently. Elliot Jay Stocks, whose piece addressing the optical size debate from a licensing perspective was featured in our [May 2025 bulletin](https://tipografi.substack.com/p/tipografi-bulteni-mays-2026), exemplifies the use of grade to balance these differences in his [article](https://elliotjaystocks.com/blog/keeping-type-consistent). Because grade, unlike weight, allows adjusting perceived darkness without substantially altering the space letters occupy or the text layout, typographic consistency and readability can be preserved across diverse production and display conditions.

While the grade axis strives to keep the space occupied by letters constant, condensed styles deliberately curtail that space. Florian Hardwig chronologically [examines](https://cjtype.com/dunbar-design-repository/articles/on-condensed-geometric-sans-serifs-by-florian-hardwig/) condensed geometric sans serifs from the 1920s to the present through examples such as Kabel, Futura, and Erbar-Grotesk. Explaining that narrow styles are particularly useful in headlines, advertising, and text requiring a compact footprint, the article argues that Dunbar’s narrow, condensed, and compressed widths unite this 1920s heritage with contemporary variable font technology.

Carrying a heritage into variable fonts also requires continuing someone else’s line. [PRINT Magazine](https://www.printmag.com/type-tuesday/gotham-variable-and-the-art-of-evolution/) covered the design process of Gotham Variable—which we announced in our [May 2026 bulletin](https://tipografi.substack.com/p/tipografi-bulteni-mays-2026) as released for Gotham’s 25th anniversary—in an interview with Sara Soskolne. Soskolne explains how she maintained the logic of the original designers while developing new intermediate styles, the limits of her own design autonomy, and the human type designer’s position in the face of AI-assisted production tools. The pre-Now26 interview in which Gotham’s designer Tobias Frere-Jones stated that AI is unnecessary in typography was also featured in our [May 2026 bulletin](https://tipografi.substack.com/p/tipografi-bulteni-mays-2026). As someone else draws new styles today for the family he designed, the question arises of how the boundary between fidelity to the original design and designer autonomy is defined.

## From Archive to Matrix, Stone to Sign

*Image: [FH Paragraaf](https://www.typografische.com/typefaces/fh-paragraaf) © Fatih Hardal*

For anyone seeking to preserve a designer’s logic, the first task is to access the material through which that logic can be traced. Stephen Kelman’s [Tschichold Archive](https://tschicholdarchive.com/) project has made accessible 2,184 works from Jan Tschichold’s archive spanning from 1918 to 1974.

As Tschichold’s archive becomes navigable, there were also typefaces in Italy from the same era that the history of typography has largely overlooked. Luca Lattuga’s archival and collection research, conducted over more than a decade, systematically brings together these typefaces produced in Italy’s small print shops and foundries between the 1920s and 1940s in the book [Alfabeti Modernisti Italiani](https://www.oakknoll.com/pages/books/142465/luca-lattuga/alfabeti-modernisti-italiani). Bearing modernist, geometric, decorative, and at times vernacular characteristics, these typefaces reveal an original typographic repertoire when read collectively. CAST Type Foundry, on the other hand, reinterpreted 25 typefaces from the collection unearthed by Lattuga’s research for the digital realm, publishing them as a collection reusable in contemporary graphic design and typography practice under the title [Alfabeti Modernisti Type Specimens](https://www.oakknoll.com/pages/books/142466/cast-cooperativa-anonima-servizi-tipografici/alfabeti-modernisti-type-specimens-ediz-ilusstrata).

Meanwhile, an undigitized branch of the Italian type tradition continues on its path with lead. Preparing printing forms through diverse methods, Dafi Kühne makes extensive use of the Ludlow type casting machine, especially for small point sizes in his posters. Having transported pallet loads of casting matrices belonging to the Nebitype system—produced by the Nebiolo type foundry in Turin and exceptionally rare today—to Switzerland, Kühne is developing a conversion stick with a local machinist to be able to use these original typefaces on the Ludlow. As we learn from Kühne’s [newsletter](https://mailchi.mp/babyinktwice/update-10?e=cd78b8bb64), the designer, who is nearing a solution to the incompatibility between the systems, plans to feature undigitized, original Italian characters in his posters soon. We have previously covered Nebiolo three times in our bulletin. In [May 2022](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-haberleri-may%C4%B1s-2022-c258249bd466), we touched upon an article by Riccardo Olocco analyzing archival materials that survived after Nebiolo’s closure. In [August 2023](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-b%C3%BClteni-a%C4%9Fustos-2023-5ee8b04702ec), we announced the publication of a research book documenting the foundry’s archival, technical, and artistic heritage, and in [October 2025](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-b%C3%BClteni-ekim-2025-803661d110ac), we brought revival discussions into the present through Olocco’s ISType Mono talk. This time, the revival debate continues on a machinist’s workbench.

At the other end of the discussion stands [FH Paragraaf](https://www.typografische.com/typefaces/fh-paragraaf), a serif typeface by [Fatih Hardal](https://www.typografische.com/) that does not adhere strictly to historical models and draws inspiration from the calligraphic roots of Dutch typography. In a process that began in 2020, referencing Hendrik van den Keere’s *Canon Romain* specimen alongside the work of Gerrit Noordzij, Bram de Does, and Jan van Krimpen, the typeface was developed from formal analyses conducted between 2020 and 2026. Reinterpreting the expressive qualities of classical calligraphy for modern publishing and digital rendering, the typeface is a robust text face with its sturdy construction and high legibility…

Does the pen determine the letterform, or the stone? In PampaType’s two-part article, Tere Schultz puts forward a hypothesis regarding the origin of Tuscan letters. The [first part](https://blog.pampatype.com/tuscan-letters-1) extends from 19th-century Tuscan typefaces to Roman epigraphy, discussing previous work by Nicolete Gray and Rob Roy Kelly. Schultz argues that the optical effects created by light, shadow, dust, and erosion on stone-carved inscriptions in cemeteries could make ordinary Roman capitals appear like Tuscan letters, suggesting a potential connection between lapidary inscriptions and Tuscan forms. In the discussion, the *Damasian* or *Philocalian* calligraphy of the 4th-century Roman calligrapher Furius Dionysius Philocalus serves as an early example. The [second part](https://blog.pampatype.com/tuscan-letters-2) brings together vernacular examples extending from Paris to Mexico (specifically Coyoacán), and from Buenos Aires’s “fileteado porteño” tradition to boat lettering in the Brazilian Amazon. This is accompanied by research on the use of Tuscan letters in South America by Priscila Lena Farias and Edna Cunha Lima, as well as the [Floating Letters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZhjOb3JodCU) video project conducted by Fernanda Martins on Amazon boat lettering artisans.

The traces of artisans are sought on land as well. Under the hashtag [#typographictourism](https://www.instagram.com/explore/search/keyword/?q=%23typographictourism), Yorgo Tloupas photographs and archives handmade signboards across regions such as Europe and Mexico. In his article published in [Wallpaper*](https://www.wallpaper.com/design-interiors/corporate-design-branding/typographic-tourism-yorgo-tloupas), he explains how the craft of traditional sign painters declined with the proliferation of personal computers and graphics software, yet original signs still survive in certain cities such as Turin, Genoa, Mérida, and Campeche.

## Custom Letters for Identity: Bar, Title Sequence, Sign Language, and Jersey

ONBOX studio placed handwriting—which computers had diminished—at the core of visual identity. At an event, the studio had over 100 guests individually handwrite the letters forming the brand name, developing a modular logo from them. It carried the irregular form and texture of the handmade letters across the other identity elements, from menus to coasters, and from signboards to the website. In the article in [PRINT Magazine](https://www.printmag.com/branding-identity-design/bar-supernova-onbox-brand-identity), Adam Hartley of ONBOX explains that in organizing the process, they prioritized authenticity over perfection, balancing the resulting raw energy through the use of typography and whitespace.

If one way to balance raw energy is typography and whitespace, another is not touching it at all. Templo derived the letterforms in DeafCity Hub’s identity from hand gestures in British Sign Language using motion capture technology. By preserving the rough and irregular forms, an authentic character was imparted to the identity. [Creative Boom](https://www.creativeboom.com/news/asking-what-typeface-deafcity-hub-used-for-its-logo-is-the-wrong-question/) notes that due to this approach, asking which typeface was used for the logo is the wrong question.

In contrast to an identity where asking about the typeface is deemed the wrong question, there is also work that turns a logo into a full typeface. Teddy Blanks, co-founder of the studio Chips, shares the background of the title sequence designs he created for films such as Barbie, Weapons, and Werwulf in [an interview](https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/teddy-blanks-chips-film-title-typography-graphic-design-spotlight-240826). He relates that he redrew the Barbie logo using high-resolution images sourced from a late-1980s magazine into a complete font, and developed a typeface inspired by a 14th-century manuscript for Werwulf. The interview also explores how typographic decisions in projects such as The Green Knight and Nickel Boys establish connections with the film narrative.

While on film the letter serves the narrative, on the back of a jersey the letter must first be legible from across the pitch. Developed by [Fatih Hardal](https://www.instagram.com/p/DcgYZysjALn/) for Beerschot jerseys as part of Nova’s evolving visual language, the name and number design presents a distinctive character with its proportions, weight, chamfered corners, and rounded details. The design strikes a balanced relationship between long-distance legibility on the pitch and Nova’s distinctive character.

One way to extend this balance established on the back of a jersey across an entire club identity is assigning roles to letters. Milan-based Alkemy+ developed a new brand strategy and visual identity system for Palermo FC based on a listening process lasting over a year. As reported by [The Brand Identity](https://the-brandidentity.com/project/over-a-year-of-listening-shaped-alkemy-s-identity-for-palermo-fc), the club’s existing crest and pink-and-black colors were retained. The Palermo Mosaico family, forming the typographic core of the project, was drawn divided into three roles under the design direction of Guido Scolaro and Andrea Biggio. Mosaico Serif assumes the symbolic role, *Mosaico Flare* the editorial and display role, and *Mosaico Sans* the system layer. While Rosa Palermo was retained as a fixed reference point in the color palette, colors derived from the city—such as Blu Mediterraneo, Verde Favorita, Oro Barocco, and Marmo Siciliano—were added to the system. In our article [“Letters on the Pitch”](https://medium.com/tipografi/sahadaki-harfler-spor-markalamas%C4%B1nda-tipografinin-y%C3%BCkselen-g%C3%BCc%C3%BC-8f249d1300ad) published in 2025, we touched upon the aesthetic and symbolic value gained by clubs establishing identities through custom typefaces. Unfortunately, despite the Turkish Football Federation (TFF) lifting the mandatory Arial requirement, no similar moves have come from clubs in Turkey.

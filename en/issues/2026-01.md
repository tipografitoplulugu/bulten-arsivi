# Typography Newsletter: January 2026

### This month's bulletin covers Volvo's new safety-focused typeface, the repercussions of the US return to Times New Roman, licensing crises in the gaming world, and developments regarding local memory.

⏰ **Reading Time:** ~4 minutes

Hello everyone!

In the January bulletin, typography's relationship with engineering-based design processes and historical documentation efforts take center stage. While legibility transforms from an aesthetic preference into measurable data on one hand, the effort to document cultural heritage and local craft amidst the velocity of digital production strengthens archival initiatives on the other.

---

## The Transformation of Type: From Aesthetics to Engineering

As Volvo prepares for its centenary celebrations in 2027, it treats typography not merely as an aesthetic preference, but directly as a safety parameter. Developed in collaboration with Dalton Maag, the new typeface *[Volvo Centum](https://www.volvocars.com/intl/news/articles/volvo-cars-introduce-typeface-designed-with-safety-in-mind/)* bears the character of an engineering project rather than just a brand identity exercise. Dalton Maag's Creative Director, Zeynep Akay, emphasizes that designing for glance-based reading in motion requires a different mindset, stating that Volvo Centum is designed to maintain its performance under pressure, across different languages, and even at speeds of 100 km/h. This approach represents one of the industrial-scale equivalents of methods based on measuring legibility through objective data, highlighted in Ellen Lupton's article titled "[The Science of Typography](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-bilimi-ellen-lupton-36ebb5196f15)" that we published in 2018. It is remarkable for demonstrating how type design has become an active component of driver safety.

While projects merging design and engineering like the Volvo example come to the fore, innovations on the software side often follow a more cautious and delayed course. The beta version of After Effects will [begin supporting](https://maketypework.com/after-effects-animate-variable-fonts/) variable fonts from the Adobe Fonts library—nearly a decade after their official introduction in 2016. This update, where axes such as weight, width, or slant turn into fluid parameters via keyframes, offers a far more flexible workflow for motion graphic designers.

The diversifying ways typography is utilized also bring legal responsibilities along with them. In [last month's bulletin](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-b%C3%BClteni-aral%C4%B1k-2025-119b6d532a2b), we featured Matthew Butterick's views that because the legal system considers fonts as computer software, copyright strategies must be shaped accordingly. The [guide](https://typeheist.co/blog/font-licensing-for-video-games/) prepared by Laura Eddy for game developers reveals the practical reality of this legal distinction. Eddy states that embedding a font file into a game engine technically and legally falls into the category of *software distribution*, making it a necessity for developers to heed the licensing difference between static images and dynamic code-based text.

While technical and legal boundaries become this clear, the wind is blowing in the exact opposite direction on the commercial side of the industry. The pursuit of perfection is giving way to experimentation. Type foundries are steering toward different strategies, moving away from the traditional *flawless* and *finished product* approach. In his newly published [article](https://revistes.uab.cat/grafica/article/view/ep-ertuk), one of our editors, Murat Ertürk, examines the industry's marketing strategies, the sale of unfinished typefaces, and the rise of experimental sub-brands. Putting pioneering actors such as Future Fonts, Zetafonts, OH no Type Foundry, and Pangram Pangram under the microscope, the study reveals how type foundries deploy their creativity while safeguarding their commercial reputations, and how they involve users in the design process.

## Cultural Memory and the Power of the Archive

While developments on the technological front continue, cultural productions delve into roots, deepening memory. In his [review](https://librekultur.com/kaleme-ve-kagida-adanmis-bir-omur/), Ömer Faruk Şerifoğlu examines the book "A Life Dedicated to Fine Arts" by Prof. Dr. İlhami Turan, a doyen of graphic design and typography. As a representative of the Emin Barın tradition, Turan's 64-year journey extending from Babıali to academia is not just a personal memoir; it serves as a historical document for Turkish typography history, graphic design education, and printing culture. A similar documentation sensitivity emerges across different geographies. Maltese designer Steven Scicluna documents the vanishing tradition of ornament and lettering on Malta's commercial vehicles with his book "[Tberfil: The Art of Maltese Vehicular Decoration in the 20th Century](https://shop.bl.ag/products/tberfil-the-art-of-maltese-vehicular-decoration-in-the-20th-century)". Both examples contribute to the literature by rendering visible, on distinct planes, the manual craftsmanship of the pre-digital era.

Historical references come to life not only in books, but also on ancient stones. In his [article](https://sei.berkeley.edu/2025/12/30/from-stone-to-screen/) exploring a type design process based on Classic Period Maya hieroglyphs and its technical details, Alexandre Bassi details the visual transformation that ancient glyphs—carved in stone with three-dimensional and complex stacking logic—underwent while being adapted to modern digital type technologies and on-screen legibility. Repercussions of US diplomacy's decision to return to Times New Roman continue. In [last month's bulletin](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-b%C3%BClteni-aral%C4%B1k-2025-119b6d532a2b), we reported on the US State Department's decision to return to Times New Roman for official correspondence. Joining this debate, Si Daniels from the Microsoft Design team offers a perspective on what switching between typefaces actually changes, [noting](https://microsoft.design/articles/we-can-all-be-friends-times-new-roman-vs-calibri/) that while both typefaces meet basic conventions in terms of readability, they convey different messages. While Daniels emphasizes that accessibility is no longer ensured solely through typeface selection but can also be achieved through reading modes offered by software, he argues that moving beyond default typefaces is necessary for genuine brand identity. Another bastion of visual memory rises from New York. Reviewed by Creative Review, the [AIGA NY poster archive](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/aiga-ny-poster-archive/) brings together works that bear witness to the last forty years of New York's graphic design scene, created by popular figures such as Stefan Sagmeister and Michael Bierut entirely for their peers, free from commercial concerns. Bearing the marks of the transition from analogue production to the digital era, this visual memory offers an experimental playground where designers pushed the boundaries of typography and composition without client pressure. At the same time, it stands out as a unique collection documenting the city's spirit of cultural and professional solidarity.

# Typography Newsletter: May 2026

### This month's bulletin features the political language of typefaces, variable font technology with optical sizing, Google Fonts' data privacy issues, and new platforms supporting independent type foundries.

⏰ **Reading Time:** ~10 minutes

Hello everyone!

The May bulletin is packed with developments demonstrating that typography is a field intersecting with artificial intelligence, surveillance, cultural memory, and production ethics. Ranging from Hellcare Regular to the Google Fonts GDPR debate, from physical archives regaining value to the history of labor, the articles remind us that typography renders visible the relations of knowledge, power, and labor well beyond design decisions.

## Artificial Intelligence, Surveillance, and Typography

*Image: [Hellcare Regular](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/parker-jones-rajshree-saraf-hellcare-regular-graphic-design-project-130526)*

Designed by Parker Jones and Rajshree Saraf, [Hellcare Regular](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/parker-jones-rajshree-saraf-hellcare-regular-graphic-design-project-130526) turns the rejection of legibility into a conscious typographic strategy. Taking the illegibility of doctors' handwriting as its reference, the font translates the bureaucratic and inaccessible structure of the American healthcare system into a visual language. The complex medical documents and forms of communication Jones encountered during his father's cancer treatment form the starting point of the font. The formal distortion is essentially a critique of the alienation generated by the healthcare system.

This approach also recalls a broader tradition in which legibility is not seen as an absolute value in typography. At the Letterform Archive, as [recounted by Zachary Sauer](https://letterformarchive.org/news/from-the-collection-legacies-of-swiss-style-part-2-wolfgang-weingart/), Wolfgang Weingart (1941–2021) developed a practice that challenged the Swiss typographic tradition from within rather than outright rejecting it, pushing the limits of modernist order through his *Typografische Monatsblätter* covers and photomechanical experiments. Both approaches demonstrate typography's power as a critical and political form of expression.

Oliver Schöndorfer [explains](https://pimpmytype.com/about-ai-design/) through the lens of AI why these approaches are even more important today. According to Schöndorfer, while typography can transform mediocre output into intentional design, establish visual hierarchy, and lend a distinctive character to a project, AI detaches the designer from thinking, and the real danger is not speed but the atrophy of judgment… [Nick Shinn](https://ilovetypography.com/2026/05/13/font-faces-nick-shinn/) stands on similar ground. Stating that drawing is a cognitive process distinct from machine learning, Shinn argues that this organic structure, grounded in muscle memory and cultural intelligence, forms an ontological barrier against artificial intelligence. Tobias Frere-Jones, in his [interview](https://typeparis.com/news/q-a-tobias-frere-jones) published ahead of the [Now26](https://typeparis.com/now26) conference, similarly remarks that AI creates an unnecessary workload in typography, while expressing that Google Fonts has had an *erosive* effect on the traditional Western market.

## The Material Layers of History

A critique of technology inevitably invites a confrontation with history. Lukas Paltram of Dalton Maag [argues](https://www.daltonmaag.com/resources/blog/2026-03-23-recasting-the-past.html) that returning to physical sources is essential to reinterpreting typography, emphasizing that the original qualities of metal typefaces on paper were largely lost during the early digitization era. As part of *The Recast Collection*, the type foundry bypasses digital files to work directly from original printed specimens, sketch drawings, and type specimens in archives. *Versum Recast*, one of the collection's members to be released in 2026, reinterprets Adrian Frutiger's 1957 neo-grotesque system as a three-axis variable font.

The return to physical sources also prepares the ground for rethinking typography's historical processes. Étienne Ghys [examines](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OIxzewWilc) how letter anatomy was constructed along a trajectory stretching from Gutenberg to Luca Pacioli and Albrecht Dürer's quest for geometric proportions, and onward to Donald Knuth's Metafont system. According to Ghys, typefaces are not merely vessels for conveying information; they are structures that represent power, transmit cultural memory, and shape visual perception. The transition from traditional craftsmanship to Bézier curves is therefore not merely a technical transformation, but a historical rupture that altered how letterforms are perceived…

The labor dimension of typography's history, which often remains in the background, becomes visible in an [essay](https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/twilight-of-the-velocipede/) adapted from Alex Wright's book "[Empire of Ink](https://alexwright.com/books/empire-of-ink/)". In the 1870s, before the Linotype had entered the scene, compositors known as *Swifts*—capable of typesetting roughly 760 words per hour—drew crowds of thousands into halls for typesetting competitions. Beyond demonstrations of technical skill, these exhibitions were public arenas where female compositors waged union struggles. The refusal of organizers to recognize the results of women who outperformed their male competitors in an 1886 competition demonstrates that the history of typography cannot be explained through a purely technological narrative.

Another trajectory of this historical layer extending to the present day is opened up by the research of Borna Izadpanah and Onur Yazıcıgil. The article titled "[Crafting Global Typography: Origins, Dissemination, and Adaptation of Naskh Types from Istanbul](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/crafting-global-typography-origins-dissemination-and-adaptation-of-naskh-types-from-istanbul/8B34BD1F60DD72A19497D4A5523D82BF)" investigates the global circulation and geographical transformations of the 24-point Ottoman Naskh printing types developed in 1867 by Ottoman-Armenian punchcutter Oḥannes Mühendisyan and court calligrapher Mustafa İzzet Efendi. While bringing to light a vital lineage of production sidelined by Western-centric typographic historiography, the study reminds us that typography must also be read across the dimensions of cultural circulation, labor, and political representation, far beyond formal matters alone.

## Licensing, Privacy, and the Cost of Free

*Image: [adobe.design](https://adobe.design/ideas/the-old-typography-is-new-again)*

Jürgen Siebert and Ivo Gabrowitsch directly [address](https://fontwerk.com/en/text/retail-fonts-vs-free-fonts) the issue of typographic monoculture caused by free typeface services like Google Fonts. The adoption of Roboto and Open Sans by millions of companies creates an effect of invisibility that progressively erodes distinctiveness in digital environments. According to the authors, free typefaces carry severe limitations in technical support, long-term maintenance, language coverage, and brand authenticity. The legal dimension of this debate came to the fore when integrating the Google Fonts infrastructure into websites via an external API resulted in visitors' IP addresses and browser data being transmitted to corporate servers without consent. A 2022 ruling by a Munich court defined this data flow as a clear violation of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), compelling website administrators to host typefaces on local servers. It remains difficult to know how many websites still operate in ignorance of this ruling. Moreover, because scanning local typeface directories on user devices plays a uniquely identifying role in browser fingerprinting, digital typography also turns into a data security concern.

Against this backdrop, new structures supporting independent type foundries are taking shape. Developed by [NaN Type Foundry](https://www.nan.xyz/), [fonts.xyz](https://www.fonts.xyz/) has been announced as a new platform bringing independent type foundries together under one roof. The platform's most notable feature is a single license model covering print, web, app, advertising, and social media use. Promising to pay designers 80% of sales in royalties, the platform also offers the possibility of building foundry-specific websites. As in all platform economies, whether this model is genuinely sustainable will only become apparent through long-term distribution and revenue practices.

Elliot Jay Stocks [connects](https://adobe.design/ideas/the-old-typography-is-new-again) the optical size discussion to the licensing agenda from a different angle. He notes that glyphs, which were designed separately for small and large point sizes during the era of movable type, have regained significance in the digital environment thanks to variable font technology. Design applications and browsers can adapt letterforms automatically or manually according to font size, making typography more responsive to context. This technical flexibility also highlights the difference between free typefaces that offer limited axes and features and advanced variable font systems.

Type.today published an in-depth [interview](https://type.today/en/journal/bulac) with Benjamin Guichard, scientific director of the Paris-based library [BULAC](https://www.bulac.fr/), on non-Western writing systems. With its extensive collection, BULAC hosts research projects ranging from developing machine learning models for Arabic manuscripts to designing fictional writing systems with students from École Estienne. The interview discusses why type designers and libraries need each other and what an independent library focused on non-Western publications means today. It serves as a reminder of how little documented the intersection of type design and library practice remains.

## In Small Print

📚 Surfaces Utiles published the monograph titled "[Velvetyne Saved My Life](https://surfaces-utiles.org/velvetyne-saved-my-life.html)", focusing on the Velvetyne collective.

🖋️ Three ghost signs on a building facade in Boston's West End neighborhood have been [restored](https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/05/19/ghost-signs-massachusetts-boston-holt-bugbee-west-end) through a collaboration between [WMS Brothers](https://www.instagram.com/wmsbrothers/), Holt & Bugbee, and the West End Museum.

🔡 Gotham celebrates its 25th anniversary with [Gotham Variable](https://www.monotype.com/company/press-release/monotype-celebrates-25-years-gotham-launch-gotham-variable), developed by Monotype.

🛠️ Roel Nieskens's [Wakamai Fondue](https://pixelambacht.nl/2026/a-new-wakamai-fondue/) tool, which we featured in our [February 2021 bulletin](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-haberleri-%C5%9Fubat-2021-6b4d000da517), has been released in a new version after a long hiatus.

💻 High-Logic released version 16 of [FontCreator](https://www.high-logic.com/news/fontcreator-16-released).

📱 [Pica](https://pica.joshpuckett.me/), a font management app developed for macOS by Josh Puckett, has been released.

🌐 Developed by Carl's Type, [FontBob](https://fontbob.com/home) has been made available as a browser-based typeface editor.

🗂️ Created by Mark Johnson of Trash Type, [Type.lol](https://type.lol/) offers tools for building collections and visualizing typographic networks.

📖 Pablo Lara Henríquez's [typography resource list](https://typography.pablolarah.cl/) brings together a wide range of content, from type foundries to tools, podcasts to historical archives.

# Typography Newsletter: August 2026

### This month's bulletin features AI manifestos, new publications on typography history, typefaces inspired by urban lettering, and growing visual identity work in football.

⏰ **Reading Time:** ~10 minutes

Hello everyone!

This month's developments revolve around the question of where typography draws its sources from. On the one hand, typefaces inspired by urban lettering take center stage, while on the other, the number of visual identity projects in football is steadily increasing. On the production front, attitudes toward artificial intelligence are noticeably diverging. While some type foundries publish manifestos championing human labor, certain formats turn AI directly into the manifesto itself. On the history front, a series of publications—ranging from metal type casting to multi-script empires and post-colonial printing struggles—reveals how established typographic principles can be called into question.

---

## Artificial Intelligence, Consent, and the Limits of Production

Type foundries' stances on artificial intelligence emerged this month through three distinct approaches. Mass Driver [announced](https://mass-driver.com/article/from-human-hands) that it does not use artificial intelligence at any stage of its design and production process. The manifesto-like text frames type design as a field of production tied to human labor, experience, and thought processes. TypeType, while acknowledging that AI accelerates design production, [argues](https://typetype.medium.com/why-unique-fonts-matter-even-in-the-age-of-ai-generated-design-2cc7c2bdbb36) that original, custom-designed typefaces play a critical role in helping brands maintain their distinctive identities in the face of widespread AI-generated design. While one side rejects AI entirely, the other accepts it as a tool but places the human designer and their originality at the heart of design. The third approach is seen in [ShieldFont](https://www.creativebloq.com/design/fonts-typography/type-designers-have-created-a-free-font-that-poisons-ai), jointly developed by Seneda & Abrucio and Playtype. Appearing ordinary to the human eye yet presenting different words to AI web scrapers, this open-source typeface operates using OpenType glyph substitution. In controlled tests, 55.8 percent of the protected passages did not convey the same factual claim as the original text. The developers define ShieldFont not as "anti-AI," but as a de facto opt-out option guided by the principle "Publishing is not consent!" Rather than rejecting or embracing AI, consent is turned into a technical defense mechanism.

[NeuralType](https://elih.net/blog/neuraltype/), developed by Eli Heuer, moves in the exact opposite direction of these approaches. In this experimental format with an NTF extension, the font file itself is a neural network, and each letterform is generated based on context. The format aims to provide an alternative to OpenType's static glyph table-based approach, especially for cursive and context-sensitive writing systems such as Nastaliq. Developed as a demo, the typeface was built as a 1.36-million-parameter multilayer perceptron (MLP) trained on the forms of the open-font-licensed Gulzar typeface.

In contrast to this scenario where type is automatically generated from context, Dan Hollick [likens](https://www.makingsoftware.com/chapters/how-to-make-a-font) typeface production to an iterative process consisting of research, conceptual decisions, character set construction, metric adjustments, testing, and iteration. A successful typeface is determined not so much by individually aesthetic glyphs, but by how characters work consistently and legibly within text. This necessitates the constant testing of design decisions. While automation can increase speed, the evaluation process continues to require human judgment.

This limitation of automation is exemplified by the [web tool](https://blode.co/static-to-variable) developed by Matthew Blode. Blode's tool generates variable fonts by interpolating between uploaded static weights. When the output is inspected in type design software, the curves turn out to consist of straight line segments rather than true curves. Although this is not noticeable to the eye, technical quality is inevitably compromised.

Aiming to catch such issues earlier during the proofing stage, [Hellbox](https://hellbox.com/) is introduced as a macOS-exclusive app that monitors Glyphs files and generates updated PDF proof sheets with every save. Offered with a one-time $100 license, the tool also enables tasks such as generating proofs and inspecting fonts via natural language through AI assistants, thanks to its MCP support. It thereby aims to catch details that might otherwise be overlooked in font production earlier and make the quality control process more continuous. From another perspective, Hellbox establishes a new layer of control supporting human oversight in an environment where artificial intelligence is increasingly integrated into the production process.

## The Technical and Political Layers of History

The principles of typography laid down by Jan Tschichold nearly a century ago have been brought back to the table. Published by Sorry Press, Matter Of's book "[New New Typography](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/matter-of-new-new-typography-graphic-design-publication-project-150726)" questions whether these principles still define contemporary design. Bringing historical examples together with contemporary typographic experiments, the work proposes an experimental and pluralistic anti-guide that treats typography not as a set of rigid rules, but within a web of political, cultural, and technological relations. Not a rulebook, but a way of questioning the rules…

To understand how and under what technical conditions these typographic rules emerged, however, one must look further back to the material production processes of type. R. Stanley Nelson's book "[The Typefounder's Hand Mould](https://www.oakknoll.com/pages/books/143247/r-stanley-nelson/typefounders-hand-mould-the)" examines in detail the history, fabrication, and use of the hand mould that made metal type casting possible in the history of Western printing. The result of more than fifty years of research, the book is presented as the first comprehensive monograph in English to provide a practical explanation of mould-making and typecasting processes alongside historical examples.

[Vicki Tinnel's article](https://typography.guru/journal/the-evolution-of-japanese-typography-from-the-edo-period-to-today-r83/) traces the history of multi-script writing systems in Japanese typography. Spanning from the Edo period to the present day, this history centers on the Tsukiji Type Foundry established during Meiji-era Westernization and Motogi Shozo. The co-existence of Chinese-derived characters, Japan's two native syllabaries, and Latin letters, along with debates over vertical and horizontal text direction, are examined through examples such as the multilingual design crisis at the 1998 Nagano Olympics.

Kathleen Meaney moves beyond the crisis of multilingual design to reveal the far harsher political history behind it. In her [article](https://www.typotheque.com/articles/from-a-trail-of-type-to-the-trail-of-tears), Meaney explores the journey from the Cherokee syllabary developed by Sequoyah in the 1820s, through its adaptation into typography, to the publication of the *Cherokee Phoenix* newspaper. She examines this process through the typographic forms of the era, situated at the intersection of the Cherokee Nation's efforts to preserve its cultural and political autonomy and the pressures of missionary work, Christianization, and assimilation policies. While the story is interrupted in 1834 when the printing press was seized by the Georgia militia, it continues with the re-establishment of Cherokee printing operations in the West following the 1838 Trail of Tears.

A record taking typography history beyond the printed page comes from David Loeb Weiss's 1978 documentary "[Farewell: Etaoin Shrdlu](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1MGjFKs9bnU)". Presenting the preparation of The New York Times' last issue printed with hot metal typesetting on July 2, 1978, the documentary chronicles the production environment of the era—from Ludlow and Linotype machines to proofing and page make-up. The replacement of Linotype by phototypesetting and electronic systems is recorded as a witness to the end of the mechanical typesetting era.

## From Street to Archive: Typographic Sources of Identity

The Noble Signs shop, which produces handcrafted signs for local businesses in New York, is the subject of an eponymous [documentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4cAt2CAptkw&t=134s). Following the workshop as it seeks to preserve endangered signs under the roof of a museum, the documentary treats sign making as an integral part of the city's visual language.

In Lagos, this role was taken a step further, directly into public sculpture. Lagos-based artist Dennis Osadebe transformed the hand-painted lettering adorning minibuses into monumental steel sculptures in the [This Is Lagos](https://www.printmag.com/type-tuesday/this-is-lagos-turns-the-citys-hand-painted-typography-into-monumental-public-art/) project. Having developed a typeface named *Godspeed* from this street typography through months of field research, the first of Osadebe's five sculptures have been installed in the cities of Ikeja, Owode, and Berger.

This street-level material became the direct source of a corporate identity in Berlin. Stan Hema's branding for the Berlin Museum [establishes](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/stan-hema-berlin-museum-graphic-design-project-060826) a dynamic logo system fed by the city's own typographic fabric rather than a singular, static logo. The system draws from diverse urban sources ranging from U-Bahn station signage and Café Moskau's Moskau Grotesk on Karl-Marx-Allee to Futura Black Stencil found on post-war Charlottenburg signs and Berlin's Späti culture. Rather than a single uniform typographic voice, the city's various historical and cultural strata converge within the same visual repertoire.

The source of corporate identity can sometimes narrow down to a single historic letter. As part of its new visual identity developed with Societas, Werder Bremen created a custom club typeface called [Bruckmeyer Neue](https://www.werder.de/en/news/other-news/2026-2027/visual-identity-30072026), inspired by Günther Bruckmeyer's 1924 letter W. Developed around the values the club defines through the bold, human, down-to-earth concept of "plietsch", the new visual identity will be phased in over two to three years starting with the 2026–2027 season.

The French Football Federation's visual identity project, on the other hand, relies not on a single letter but on a pair of type families serving two distinct functions. Solstices Studio and Blaze Type designed the custom type families [France and Héritage](https://blazetype.eu/case-studies/fff-france-heritage/) for the national teams. In the eight-member system, *France* handles contemporary and functional communications, while *Héritage* carries applications tied to history, achievements, and memory.

The same interplay of memory and contemporaneity finds a formal solution in Jamie Clarke's [Reel](https://www.printmag.com/type-tuesday/how-all-caps-lost-its-voice/) typeface. Combining the visual impact of capitals with the more human and conversational tone of lowercase letters, Clarke designed the lowercase characters to occupy the same visual area and density as uppercase letters. In response to the eroded power of all-caps usage, he proposes a headline voice that is more open and inviting of discussion.

The research behind tone sometimes reaches straight into the archives. In its identity for eyewear brand [Bonnie Clyde](https://mouthwash.studio/project/bonnie-clyde/), Mouthwash Studio conducted extensive research drawing from the Gutenberg Bible, Shakespeare texts, and typography archives to design a custom logo (wordmark). Drawing on resources from the Herb Lubalin Study Center and the New York Public Library, the work adapts historical letterforms into a contemporary brand language. The project demonstrates how typographic research emerges not merely as a reference, but as a methodology directly shaping design decisions.

## Small Points

🔍 FontBase expanded typeface discovery with two data tools. While [Fontalog](https://fontalog.xyz/) makes over 3.5 million typefaces searchable by visual characteristics such as contrast, x-height, proportion, and weight, [Font Rankings](https://fontba.se/tools/rankings) ranks the most widely used typefaces daily and monthly based on usage data.

🧩 Developed by Chingru, [FontColle](https://font.chingru.com/) provides dedicated filters for Google Fonts by OpenType features, variable font axes, writing systems, and color vs. monochrome distinctions; [discussions on TypeDrawers](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/5655/fontcolle-an-enhanced-google-fonts-filter) reveal that Unicode character filtering clashes with gaps in Google's subset definitions.

🎓 The [Type and Media](https://2026.typemedia.org/) master's program at the Royal Academy of Art, The Hague has published its 2025–2026 graduation projects.

♿ On August 13, Type.today [published](https://type.today/en/journal/hector-mangas) an interview with cognitive psychologist and Accessibility Tools founder Héctor Mangas. Mangas reports that in their survey of over a hundred designers, the biggest barrier to accessibility was aesthetic concern, followed by time and cost, and that most typefaces advertised as dyslexia-friendly have no backing in the scientific literature.

🗣️ Yaprak Buse Çağlar will deliver a talk titled "Designing against forgetting" at [Rencontres de Lure](https://delure.org/les-rencontres/toutes-les-rencontres/rencontres-2026), to be held in France on August 22–27. Through the Aksan typeface, developed with inspiration from Turkey's Alphabet Reform, and research conducted at ANRT, she transforms historical forms into contemporary typographic systems.

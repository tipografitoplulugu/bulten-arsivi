# Typography Newsletter: April 2026

### This month's bulletin features Fontstand's 1,300-participant licensing survey, the creative frontiers of variable font technology, typography research, and history.

⏰ **Reading Time:** ~9 minutes

Hello everyone!

In April, the typography world reveals that seemingly independent issues share a common tension. Market data demonstrating how licensing complexity restricts deliberate design choices, texts discussing variable font technology moving beyond technical flexibility onto an aesthetic and theoretical ground, and studies questioning typography's relationship with identity and culture through research programs intersect on common ground. Meanwhile, an overlooked relic of printing history offers an unexpected reading into the origins of the discipline.

## Licensing Complexity and Market Behavior

*Image: [Fontstand 2025 survey results.](https://fontstand.com/news/design-news/survey/)*

The [results of the survey,](https://fontstand.com/news/design-news/survey/) conducted by Fontstand throughout 2025 and answered by over 1,300 respondents, turned a long-debated tension in the type industry into concrete data. 93.8% of participants prefer one-time purchases over subscription models, citing the ability to re-access files in long-term projects and avoid losing access when a license expires. While Adobe Fonts maintains its dominant position in subscription usage because it is bundled with the Creative Cloud subscription, this rate drops to 8.8% when it comes to conscious choice.

Behind this pattern of preference lies a structural problem with the licensing system. Nearly two-thirds of respondents state that they have abandoned purchasing a typeface at least once due to complex or ambiguous terms. The most frequently voiced complaint is the requirement for distinct licenses across web, print, and application uses. Designers note turning to Google Fonts to avoid the difficulty of explaining these distinctions during client meetings. These numbers clearly demonstrate that lost revenue stems not from price, but from confusion. While design quality leads typeface selection at 94%, clear licensing follows closely at 59% when asked about expectations from subscription services, right behind affordable pricing at 64%.

In an environment where the licensing system so heavily constrains user decisions, Latinotype's release of Willy Caslon brings a different question to the fore. When expectations for quality and functionality rise, must revival projects adapt as well? [According to Ameel Khan's review,](https://www.insanityworks.org/randomtangent/2026/4/12/willy-caslon-an-excellent-caslon-revival) this work resolves the imbalances inherited from the original movable type era—which the vast majority of existing digital versions of Caslon deliberately preserve—making the typeface more functional in modern publishing contexts.

## Technical and Conceptual Boundaries of Variable Fonts

*Image: [Axes in a variable font](https://contrastfoundry.com/projects/how-to-design-a-variable-font).*

Current discussions around variable fonts are no longer confined to the expansion of technical capacity, increasingly moving toward a multi-layered ground encompassing aesthetic, theoretical, and industrial dimensions. Aleksandra Samuļenkova's [article](https://tdc.org/news/movement-matters-variable-fonts-new-tonal-expressions/) examining six projects within the context of the TDC72 Typeface Design Competition that transform the variable font beyond technical flexibility into an expressive medium where motion is woven into the essence of the design, alongside Contrast Foundry's [article](https://contrastfoundry.com/projects/how-to-design-a-variable-font) exploring unconventional axis uses—such as stencil effects, motion simulation, and pattern-based experiments in addition to classical axes like weight, width, and slant—materialize the creative possibilities of the variable font format, offering a powerful reference for both type designers and designers closely following this technology. These developments find concrete reflection in applications demonstrating how variable fonts redefine the balance between legibility and flexibility in large-scale system design, as seen in the [Monaspace](https://lettermatic.com/custom/monaspace-case-study) project developed in collaboration between Lettermatic and GitHub. While providing flexibility along weight, width, and slant axes, Monaspace aims to resolve legibility issues inherent to monospace typefaces through an approach dubbed *Texture Healing*.

When the debate descends to the interface level, Michael Rafailyk, in his critique of the Liquid Glass design language, [argues](https://michaelrafailyk.com/blog/liquidglassfromatypographyperspective) that the optical size principle has not sufficiently translated into interface design, and that while glass material succeeds on large surfaces, it creates contrast and clarity issues in small-scale icons. As a solution, he suggests designing icons with scale-sensitive variations, parallel to the logic of optical size in variable fonts.

## Research, History, and Identity: Typography's Theoretical Ground

Typotheque, as a type foundry with a full-time scientist on staff, is currently conducting three distinct research studies simultaneously. Aiming to rank the foundry's catalog through crowdsourced participation, the [Font Expressiveness Game](https://typotheque.qualtrics.com/jfe/form/SV_1T6LwPLfIeIdzsG?utm_source=newsletter_155&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=2026-03-25&utm_content=FontExpressivenessGame) presents users with pairs of typefaces and asks which feels more expressive. A field study conducted in Hyderabad in collaboration with Urdu University and the Center for Urdu Language [investigates](https://typotheque.qualtrics.com/jfe/form/SV_dd0PEgjOynP5kaO) how different Nastaliq styles are perceived by readers of Urdu, Arabic, and Persian. A [survey](https://typotheque.qualtrics.com/jfe/form/SV_3rX0mXjScVJsrKS) exploring user preferences and needs regarding accessibility in design, in collaboration with Matthew Baxter, is also ongoing. Participation in all three surveys remains open.

Alongside this landscape of current perception research, Sara Rosinsky examines an overlooked relic of printing history. Because Linotype typesetting machines lacked a delete function, operators would run their fingers down the first two vertical columns of the keyboard to mark erroneous lines. The fact that these warning lines slipped through during hectic press runs and appeared in published newspapers turned the phrase "[Etaoin Shrdlu](https://creativepro.com/do-you-know-etaoin-shrdlu/)" into one of the most recognized textual artifacts in printing history.

For those wishing to trace the origins of the Swiss Style from primary sources, Zachary Sauer of Letterform Archive wrote about the archive's [Typografische Monatsblätter](https://letterformarchive.org/news/legacies-of-swiss-style-tyografische-monatsblatter/) (TM) collection. Positioning TM as both a commercial trade publication and a forum where modernist typography was debated, this article is rich in visual material, drawing directly from archival assets with cover designs and page layouts. Within the same historical framework, the book titled "[Lettres Décoratives](https://www.typeroom.eu/the-soul-of-the-french-street-letterform-archive-unveils-lettres-dcoratives)," published by Letterform Archive, compiles specimen and lettering albums used by sign painters in France between 1830 and 1930, bringing visibility to a poorly documented layer of the pre-industrial lettering tradition.

In an [article](https://surfacepodcast.substack.com/p/typography-is-more-than-organizing) written on Nicole Killian's book "[Identity is Variable](https://polymodesans.com/book/)," it is argued that typographic design is not merely a formal arrangement of text, but a process of understanding the identities of the subjects who read and interact with that text. By providing an analytical foundation for how letterforms and typesetting choices function within a sociocultural context, the article questions traditional boundaries within the typography discipline and brings a theoretical framework to this discussion. Comprising 37 essays by 43 authors, "[The Bloomsbury Handbook of Global Typography](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/bloomsbury-handbook-of-global-typography-9781350336384/)" expands this discussion across geographic and historical breadths. Borna Izadpanah's chapter, tracing the transformation of Nastaliq script into metal type in nineteenth-century Istanbul and Cairo, reevaluates Ottoman and Egyptian print culture through the lens of typographic history.

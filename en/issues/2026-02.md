# Typography Newsletter: February 2026

### This month's bulletin highlights the rise of direct sales in the type market, the role of legibility in cybersecurity, modernist archive projects, and new state identities.

⏰ **Reading Time:** ~6 minutes

Hello everyone!

The February bulletin focuses on economic, technical, and historical topics in the world of typography. Type designers are turning to their own websites to reduce their dependence on traditional marketplaces and monopolistic structures. At the same time, legibility research and cybersecurity discussions frame fundamental typographic challenges in a new light. Meanwhile, archive projects bring the field's historical legacy back to the forefront by shedding light on early twentieth-century theoretical debates and lettering practices across different geographies.

---

## A Shift in the Type Market and Monopolization Debates

In a [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MsPlT9KQEKU) video, Harbor Bickmore shares 2025 sales data, pointing to a trend toward professional and direct sales in the type market. While revenues in hobby-oriented marketplaces like Creative Market and Etsy have plummeted dramatically, Monotype revenues and direct sales through the designer's own website are seeing record increases. As clients avoiding the subscription economy turn toward custom type design commissions, this revenue stream's share of total turnover has reached massive proportions; Bickmore advises designers aiming for a sustainable career to invest in personal type foundry websites supported by Behance and email lists, rather than intermediary platforms. Similarly, a [recent report](https://typenetwork.com/articles/font-sales-figures-type-report) published by Type Network provides concrete data demonstrating that designers focus on aesthetics and quality rather than price when selecting fonts. Of the designers participating in the study, 89.5% stated that they look primarily at overall aesthetics and style before licensing a font, followed by the breadth of the type family. Pricing and licensing terms, however, lag far behind in the decision-making process. Designers turn directly to type foundries' websites 72.4% of the time when searching for fonts, and their motivation to purchase is influenced far more by personal appreciation for the font or admiration for the designer than by discounts. This trend toward independence is also reshaping revenue models across the industry. For instance, in response to declining designer shares, [Font Bros](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/4859/65-now-and-forever-font-bros) is offering a system that proposes a 65% royalty rate for independent type foundries and provides a free website-building infrastructure.

As the commercial structure of the market shifts in this direction, anti-monopoly pushback is also intensifying. We previously addressed the debates fueled by Monotype's acquisition of independent type foundries in our article titled "[Monopolization in the Type Industry](https://medium.com/tipografi/yazı-endüstrisinde-tekelleşme-f100fe18760b)." A new development following up on these debates has emerged. LettError founder Erik van Blokland announced that although his contract with Monotype ended on April 1, 2023, the company continued to sell and license certain typefaces through Monotype, FontShop, and MyFonts channels. Stating that his emails to Monotype on the matter went unanswered, van Blokland launched a website called [Post Mono](https://letterror.com/postmono/) to materialize his protest, embedding a statement directly within the site's code. Furthermore, in a [statement made on LettError's website](https://letterror.com/fonts/fontfonts.html), he declared that the type foundry would never be sold to such an entity. He also emphasized that anyone who purchased any of the typefaces in question through these channels or used them under a subscription after January 1, 2024 does not hold a valid license and must renew their license directly through LettError. In this increasingly complex commercial ecosystem, Doug Wilson, who has a track record of working with global brands, announced [Type Advisor](https://typeadvisor.com/), a typography-focused consulting platform. Developed for creative studios and type foundries, the platform offers expert support in technical areas such as type library strategy, market research, licensing agreement guidance, and accessibility standards. Alongside technical consulting, Wilson aims to bring a holistic consulting approach to the typography industry's complex business processes by providing content-oriented solutions such as copywriting, case studies, and marketing collateral design.

## Typography in the Digital Age: Legibility, Security, and Systems

In digital interfaces, typographic choices go beyond aesthetic considerations to directly affect security and performance. Charles Nix [examines](https://www.fastcompany.com/91489792/how-common-fonts-can-enable-digital-scams) how the minimalist construction of ubiquitous typefaces like Arial and Helvetica is weaponized by cyber scammers due to visually indistinguishable letterforms, arguing that legibility must be prioritized for security. These recommendations are also corroborated by David Březina's [research](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13506285.2026.2627306) published in the journal *Visual Cognition*. The study demonstrates that the perception of letter similarity depends not solely on form, but is strongly linked to type design, literacy levels, and contextual evaluations. Jessica Walsh, meanwhile, critiques the typographic monotony of brands, [arguing](https://www.creativeboom.com/insight/typography-might-be-the-last-thing-ai-cant-fake/) that the systematization process prioritizing efficiency erodes unique expression and that typography may be one of the last domains bearing the mark of the human touch in the face of artificial intelligence. On the technical performance side, HTTP Archive's Web Almanac [2025 report](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/fonts#web-font-services) documents that 72% of websites have abandoned external services like Google Fonts in favor of self-hosting their font files, a trend driven by the need to comply with privacy regulations.

Type designers are also re-examining their production tools. In the new version of his personal website, designer Rob Weychert [approaches](https://v7.robweychert.com/blog/2026/01/v7-typographic-scales-and-technical-pens/) typographic scales through a perspective inspired by analog technical drawing tools. Reminding us that before digital production, architects and draftsmen worked with technical pens that provided fixed line weights, Weychert proposes simplifying text hierarchy with a limited number of point sizes and corresponding stroke weights. The article aims to enhance visual consistency and strengthen readability across large, content-heavy pages. Noting that low-contrast typefaces are particularly well suited to this approach, he contends that typographic decisions should be conceived through the logic of tools. Similarly, Harbor Type offers a [guide](https://www.harbortype.com/how-to-animate-variable-fonts-using-python-and-drawbot/) demonstrating how variable fonts can be animated using Drawbot and Python. Finally, in an experimental typeface based on the 13th-century Cistercian numeral system, Bobbie Chen [enables](https://digitalseams.com/blog/making-a-font-with-9999-ligatures-to-display-thirteenth-century-monk-numerals) numbers to automatically transform into medieval symbols by defining 9,999 ligatures via OpenType.

## The Memory of Typography: Archives, Ideologies, and New Visual Identities

The historical and ideological roots of typography are being reintroduced to the literature through new archive projects and corporate identity initiatives. The [Typography Debate](https://typography-debate.org/) project, brought to life by Sven Fuchs, gathers digitally the manifestos and polemics that emerged around the New Typography in 20th-century Europe, reopening modernist typography to re-examination through historical sources as a site of ideological debate. Another contribution to documenting cultural transformation comes from the rare *Meishuzi* manuals added to the [Letterform Archive](https://letterformarchive.org/news/this-just-in-chinese-lettering-manuals/). These handbooks document the evolution of modern lettering practices in China's political and commercial communication between 1930 and 1971, as well as the typographic reflections of the simplified character reform. Meanwhile, "[Printing Films](https://printingfilms.com/)," founded by Doug Wilson, presents a rare archive documenting the historical evolution of typography, journalism, and printing technologies. As the heritage of the past is transferred to the web, contemporary state and institutional identities are also being reimagined through custom typographic solutions. The Czech government is renewing the visual identity of state administrations with a custom typeface called [Czechia Sans](https://www.najbrt.cz/en/news/the-visual-identity-of-the-state-administration-of-the-czech-republic), designed by Suitcase Type Foundry. A similar transformation is taking place with the [Hermitage](https://typetype.org/blog/typography-in-the-museum-how-we-developed-the-corporate-typeface-for-the-hermitage/) type family, designed by the TypeType team for the Hermitage Museum. Aiming to reflect historical context without trapping the new typefaces in the aesthetics of a specific era, the project is positioned as the first step in a comprehensive transformation that will reshape the museum's digital identity from the ground up.

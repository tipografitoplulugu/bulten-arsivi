# Typography Newsletter: October 2026

> This month's bulletin features discussions around ATypI’s Sharjah conference, typeface licensing, developments in multiscript typography, and works exploring street lettering.

**Published by:** [Turkish Typography Society](https://substack.com/@tipografi)
**Publication date:** November 1, 2026
**Source:** <https://tipografi.substack.com/p/tipografi-bulteni-ekim-2026>

Hello everyone!

October stands out in the typography world with discussions on responsibility, ownership, and the value of labor. While a conference’s speaker program sparks a boycott debate within the profession, articles on licensing models, copyright, and artificial intelligence question who owns a typeface and what determines the value of work. Multiscript typography extends from digital font libraries to subway signage and craft traditions. Street lettering, meanwhile, is reread through a design lens, from signage to manhole covers and stone inscriptions.

## Responsibility, Ownership, and the Measure of Labor

The inclusion of Alexandra Korolkova in the program of ATypI’s conference held in Sharjah, United Arab Emirates, between October 28–31, 2026, led to the withdrawal of two Ukrainian speakers. Oleksandra Korchevska-Tsekhosh and Oleksii Chekal, alongside the Ukrainian design community, sent an [open letter](https://en.lb.ua/news/2026/09/08/41322_ukrainian_designers_pull_out.html) to the ATypI Board of Directors requesting Korolkova’s removal. The letter cites Korolkova’s partnership in ParaType and the PT Astra Sans and PT Astra Serif typefaces developed for Astra Linux, used by the Russian military and security services, as justification. According to [TypeDrawers](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/5693/atypi-2026-sharjah-who-is-going), more than three hundred individuals and institutions signed the letter. John Hudson announced that he would not attend because ATypI continued to provide a platform to one of ParaType’s partners. Ongoing discussions under the [#boycottATypI](https://typo.social/tags/boycottATypI) hashtag on Typo.social came back into focus with an [essay](https://monokrom.no/essays/deafening-silence) published shortly before the conference opened. Frode Helland claimed that following the open letter, ATypI deleted critical comments on social media and blocked dissenting voices. An email dated September 19, 2026, informing Korolkova that she was still expected in Sharjah, was later [leaked](https://typo.social/@salnykov/117331087352513046). Helland reported that the board and sponsors remained silent, while public reactions were limited to an [Instagram post](https://www.instagram.com/p/De8s4q9jTVZ/) and a [Mastodon comment](https://typo.social/@stewf@mstdn.party/117485858988413667) stating that participation in the conference was restricted to its Stanford leg.

What the community applauds and what it opposes is also the subject of Agyei Archer’s [article](https://blog.agyei.design/whose-font-is-it-anyway/). Areal, which we introduced in the [August 2025 bulletin](https://medium.com/tipografi/tipografi-b%C3%BClteni-a%C4%9Fustos-2025-410f52e44a70), was developed by redrawing the letterforms from screenshots of an early version of Arial and received positive reactions from the design community. Archer compares the applause for this approach with how the same circle mostly characterizes training artificial intelligence models as *theft*. According to him, ownership is not only about who did the design, but also about against whom and to what extent one can enforce a claim. Furthermore, the license for the free version of Areal prohibits modifying the typeface, creating derivative works, and using it in AI training.

This time, a type foundry takes on the task of explaining the value of licensing. Fontwerk’s article titled [“Why should I pay for fonts?”](https://fontwerk.com/en/text/why-should-i-pay-for-fonts) addresses nine potential objections from managers one by one. It calculates the daily cost of a €3,500 license to be used over five years at approximately €1.90. Drawing on a 2021 study by Neurons and Monotype, it also reports that a well-chosen typeface can boost positive response by up to 13%, memorability by up to 10%, and trust by 9%. It emphasizes that free and open-source typefaces can also be the right choice if they meet requirements. A second article, titled [“Why license fonts?”](https://fontwerk.com/en/text/retail-fonts-vs-free-fonts), presents an eight-point list to convince the team. It highlights GDPR risks that may arise from dynamic font integration in services like Google Fonts, the reduction in distinctiveness from using the same typeface as competitors, the lack of technical support, and viewing typefaces as a mere commodity rather than a strategic brand asset. These discussions also bring the ways licenses are offered to the forefront. Blaze Type’s new subscription model is a concrete example in this regard. In the [Blaze Font Pack](https://blazetype.eu/font-subscription) subscription, licensing is defined via *project slots*. Each slot covers a single client project regardless of duration and number of deliverables, while different projects for the same client require separate slots. Even if the subscription ends, licenses for delivered projects—including websites—remain valid.

The cost of bypassing the production process, however, does not appear on the price tag. LettError’s [article](https://letterror.com/ai) on generative artificial intelligence argues that the design process is not just about producing a result, but also about developing the criteria to evaluate that result. The person engaging in the process weighs alternatives, tests the limits of the tool and the work, and learns to decide what is sufficient. When this process is bypassed, the user fails to develop the criteria needed to judge how good the resulting outcome is. According to the article, the inability to judge the output is the true appeal of these tools, and not learning is where time is saved. If education fails to impart critical thinking skills, reacquiring them later simply by looking at products is nearly impossible.

LettError’s critique linking time savings to a loss of learning also brings the value of time spent on the design process into discussion. Robin Mientjes considers this time as one of the components of design. In her [talk](https://essays.veryrobin.me/modes-of-criticism.html) titled “Modes of Criticism,” introducing Monokrom’s new website, she brings together Simone Weil’s concepts of attention, labor, and rootedness, Walter Benjamin’s question of who writes history, and Francisco Laranjo’s critique of the “It’s nice, that!” mentality. According to Mientjes, care shown to design makes humans more human and can form a stance against hyper-commercialization.

## The Multilingual World of Typography

Monotype and Canva expanded their partnership by adding over a thousand new typeface styles to Canva’s library. This brought the total number of styles on the platform from Monotype and its partner type foundries to over three thousand. According to [Monotype’s press release](https://finance.yahoo.com/media-advertising/articles/canva-monotype-bring-1-000-153000540.html), the expansion focuses on support for Arabic, Bengali, Hindi, Khmer, Lao, Thai, and Vietnamese. Meanwhile, TypeTogether released new versions for [Futura®100](https://www.typetogether.com/blog/futura100-a-truly-worldwide-phenomenon) supporting Simplified Chinese, Korean, and nine Indic scripts. Along with last year’s 12 writing systems, the family now reaches 23 writing systems, and according to TypeTogether, more than 90% of the world’s population can use the family in their own language.

On the implementation side, Ritik Shah [compared](https://designregression.com/case%20study/multilingual-directional-signage-in-indian-metros) the directional signage of nine metro systems in India in terms of script hierarchy, alignment, and typeface selection. Six systems use variants of the Devanagari typeface Yogesh dating from the 1980s–1990s, while Nagpur selects Mukta for all writing systems, and Ahmedabad chooses distinct contemporary typefaces per script (Kohinoor, Mukta, Hind). Shah argues that the lack of a standardized guideline leads to a fragmented user experience, proposing legibility testing and script-specific type design work.

The relationship between Indic scripts and digital typography was also on the agenda at [Typoday](https://typoday.org), held in Kochi from September 28–30, 2026. Organized for the 18th time, the conference took place under the theme “Typographic Intersections: Analogue to Digital and Beyond” in collaboration with the IDC School of Design at IIT Bombay. Meanwhile, in a [talk](https://geoffreybawa.com/events/kolam-an-ephemeral-quotodien-ritual-floor-art-as-a-typeface-with-ishan-khosla) delivered in Colombo, Sri Lanka, Ishan Khosla discussed how [The Typecraft Initiative](https://www.typecraftinitiative.org/), of which he is the founder, creates contemporary typefaces rooted in history and tradition alongside craftspeople, indigenous artists, and refugees. Starting from *kolam*, the ancient ritual floor patterns drawn by women on doorsteps, the team worked with 15 kolam artists in Tamil Nadu to develop kolam-based letters.

## Street Letters: Signage, Manhole Covers, and Stone Inscriptions

Jack Usine turns the technical imperfections of his self-taught type design practice into an aesthetic quality. In his [interview](https://monokrom.no/essays/on-resisting-graphic-design) with Frode Helland, he describes stepping away from commercial graphic design, setting up a studio in rural France, and the interplay between type design and lettering. He explains the origin of the [Jules Vernacular](https://vernacular.fr/) archive—which we featured in the [March 2026 bulletin](https://tipografi.substack.com/p/tipografi-bulteni-mart-2026)—through his fascination with poorly made signs and sign painters' failed letterforms. He proposes reading these imperfections as visual poetry by transposing them to different contexts and scales. In the interview, locality and collaboration take precedence over commercial success and designer heroism.

Usine seeing aesthetic possibilities in flawed letterforms also calls to mind another work focusing on the history and transformation of sign painting. In an [interview](https://bl.ag/chatting-with-morgane-come-author-of-la-peinture-en-lettres/), Morgane Côme discusses the preparation process of her book “[La peinture en lettres, Histoire & Renouveau](https://www.lechappee.org/collections/action-graphique/la-peinture-en-lettres),” which examines sign painting in France across the axes of history, technique, and revival, featuring more than five hundred images.

When molds replace the brush, however, letters become anonymous. Maryam Hosseinnia’s [article](https://www.eyemagazine.com/blog/post/farsi-cast-in-iron) examines the Persian lettering on cast-iron manhole covers in Iran in terms of typography, framing, proportion, and contrast. In examples from Tehran, Isfahan, Kashan, and Yazd, the letters range from the most elaborate forms of Nastaliq script to simple shapes that resemble typefaces. On one cover, the dots are unusually large; on some covers, manufacturer names in Latin characters appear alongside the Persian text. The designs of the Isfahan and Kashan covers are inspired by dome architecture, while the raised square grid in Kashan resembles tile patterns.

In contrast to mass-produced typefaces, there are also letters drawn from scratch for a single space. As part of the renovation of the Kloosterkerk in The Hague, LettError [designed](https://letterror.com/articles/kloosterkerk/) a custom inscription on the semicircular Belgian blue stone floor at the entrance, containing the name of the church and a two-line blessing. The inscription was arranged so it can also be read from the exit direction, with the two lines separated by the Chi Rho monogram symbolizing Jesus Christ.

## Small Print

- 📦 The [Typography Newsletter Archive](https://tipografi-bulteni-arsivi.streamlit.app/), where you can search with keywords across the bulletins we have published every month since 2019, is now live!
- 🔠 Didem Öğmen published design notes for [Manchet](https://journal.didemogmen.com/2026/09/17/background-notes-about-manchet/), which interprets 19th-century extended sans-serif styles.
- ✨ Microsoft released [Michelangelus](https://microsoft.design/articles/designing-michelangelus/), a serif typeface inspired by Michelangelo’s manuscripts.
- 🚀 Designed over more than four years for Arsenal, [Rocastle](https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/david-rudnick-johnnie-campbell-arsenal-rocastle-typeface-graphic-design-spotlight-121026) forms the club's typographic system alongside its Northbank and Chapman typefaces.
- 🧭 Richard Niessen designed a shared visual language based on a line grid for [Kanaalpark](https://dutchdesigndaily.com/stories/kanaalpark/), which runs along the Alkmaar Canal.
- 📚 The sixth issue (F) of [Footnotes](https://fontsinuse.com/uses/80393/footnotes-issue-f) journal, dated September 2026, opens with an article by Sophie Wietlisbach examining Swiss typewriter typefaces through a forensic science lens.
- 📖 Letterform Archive has opened pre-orders for the book on Joan Trochut’s modular type system [SuperTipo Veloz](https://letterformarchive.org/shop/super-veloz/), originally released in 1942.
- 🛠️ [Tegaki](https://tegaki.ink/) is an MIT-licensed open-source library supporting eight writing systems that extracts a typeface’s glyph strokes and stroke order, animating text as if it were being handwritten.
- 📖 TypeMates’ [article](https://www.typemates.com/news/schrift-und-barrierefreiheit) reports that Germany’s Accessibility Strengthening Act (BFSG) does not impose mandatory requirements on typeface selection, while the DIN 1450 standard and DBSV guidelines remain recommendations.
- 🔎 Dan Reynolds [examines](https://www.typeoff.de/2022/12/depesi-types/) the history of Depesi poster typefaces produced in Germany in the early 20th century and the manufacturing technique based on die-cutting vulcanized material and mounting it on wooden blocks.

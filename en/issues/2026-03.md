# Typography Newsletter: March 2026

**Turkish Typography Society**
April 1, 2026

### This month's bulletin features revival criticism, historical archives, corporate type designs, web typography, and current event announcements.

⏰ **Reading Time:** ~8 minutes

Hello everyone!

The March bulletin examines access to and the interpretation of historical material from multiple angles. While archival projects and documentation initiatives bring typography's reference sources back to the forefront, corporate type designs and public facade applications open typography's functional scale to debate. Meanwhile, CSS conventions and Unicode research in web typography bring overlooked dimensions of digital infrastructure to light.

## Revival Criticism, Historical Archives, and Vernacular Sources

In her [essay](https://essays.veryrobin.me/reconsidering-revivals.html) critiquing revival practices in type design, Robin Mientjes emphasizes that reproducing past designs often fosters an excessive attachment to specific names and examples rather than an understanding of design principles. According to Mientjes, the true value of historical material lies not in imitating forms, but in comprehending its underlying logic, the conditions of its era, and principles of craftsmanship.

Cambridge University Library's project presents a concrete example of facilitating access to historical material. Donated to the university in 1953 by the Deberny & Peignot Type Foundry, more than three thousand punches by John Baskerville have been analyzed using advanced imaging techniques and [made accessible online](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/collections/baskervillepunches/2). Doug Wilson's [research](https://buttondown.com/linotypebook/archive/smithsonian-discoveries/) shares the same methodological priority. Sifting through the Linotype Company archives for the book he is preparing, Wilson worked with 34 boxes of archival material, rare publications, and graphic arts collections.

In contrast to Wilson's research grounded in corporate archives, Pooja Saxena archives dozens of writing systems across India through the [India Street Lettering](https://indiastreetlettering.com) project, categorizing them by style, material, and function. One of the project's outputs, the [book](https://indiastreetlettering.com/india-street-lettering-a-journey-through-typographic-craft-culture/), brings together more than 300 photographs compiled from 17 cities and 13 writing systems. Jack Usine's [Jules Vernacular](https://vernacular.fr) archive offers a European counterpart to this systematic documentation approach, featuring over 1,700 photographs from across France.

The reflection of documentation practices in type design often stems from the designer's relationship with the local environment. Two recently released typefaces stand out as tangible examples of this relationship: [Cassis](https://frerejones.com/blog/cassis-generous-geometry), designed by Nina Stössinger inspired by Swiss and Belgian street signage, and [Buckridge](https://djr.com/notes/buckridge-font-of-the-month), developed by David Jonathan Ross based on lettering from an old trigonometry textbook. In an [interview](https://maketypework.com/making-type-amy-hood-of-hoodzpah/), Amy Hood also explained how she designs typefaces drawing from Southern California's historic signage and *Midcentury* aesthetics, noting that deciding when to finish is the most challenging stage in every project.

Beyond the relationship established with the local, the issue of ownership addressed in Samaa Khullar's article is also noteworthy. In her [article](https://www.gqmiddleeast.com/article/the-designer-behind-mamdanis-arabic-campaign-has-a-bigger-vision) centered on the visual identity of Zohran Mamdani's Arabic election campaign, Khullar demonstrates that from the invention of the printing press to keyboards and digital typefaces, technology has consistently tended to simplify writing systems and strip them of their nuances, and that Arab designers are fighting to reclaim their own writing systems.

## Type Design as Corporate Identity, Context, and Mode of Expression

Designed for Snickers, [Snickers Sans](https://studio-drama.com/projects/snickers) is a current example of how brand typography can transform into a functional system. Derived from the brand's iconic logo, the type family brings the American Gothic tradition into the brand identity. Commissioned by Swedish dairy company Arla for use across more than 120 markets and millions of packaging units, [Arla Smile](https://capdesign.se/arla-smile-ett-typsnitt-designat-for-varlden/) stands out with rounded forms and pronounced ink traps that enhance legibility. Both projects demonstrate that type design is the product of functional requirements such as scalability, multilingual support, and corporate consistency.

Monotype made headlines with two separate announcements this month. Thanks to a partnership with brand management platform Frontify, typefaces in the Monotype Fonts library can now be accessed directly within Frontify, allowing creative teams to [manage](https://www.monotype.com/company/press-release/monotype-and-frontify-streamline-workflows-font-integration) brand typography without switching platforms. In addition, the company launched its [AI Search](https://www.monotype.com/company/press-release/monotype-launches-ai-search-transforming-font-search-and-discovery-conversational-ai) tool, powered by natural language and conversational AI. Allowing users to search using phrases describing mood, brand character, or context of use, the system aims to redefine font discovery by moving away from traditional search methods.

A compelling addition was made to typographic applications in the public sphere this month. Excerpts from Obama's 2015 "You Are America" speech were rendered in the Gotham typeface on the facade of the [Obama Presidential Center](https://chicago.suntimes.com/chicago/2026/03/07/obama-presidential-center-opening-juneteenth-chicago) in Chicago, scheduled to open on June 19, 2026. We previously saw a similar example at the [University of Amsterdam Library](https://letterror.com/uva/index.html), where translations of a line by Martinus Nijhoff across 23 languages and 6 writing systems were inscribed on the library facade using a custom typeface designed specifically for the building by Erik van Blokland. Although both projects establish the relationship between architecture and typography differently, the typeface choices are not accidental; they are deliberate decisions consistent with each structure's public discourse.

In an interview with Monotype Creative Director Charles Nix, the role of typography in branding systems was [discussed](https://www.printmag.com/type-tuesday/typography-and-the-future-of-olympic-branding/) through the lens of the Milano Cortina 2026 Winter Olympics visual identity. According to Nix, the challenge is not designing a good logo, but building a robust system where even small elements—such as colors, symbols, or typographic details—can evoke the whole. The question of how local references transform into a distinctive voice and remain meaningful without falling into imitation forms the focal point of the conversation.

Neville Brody's release of the [Popaganda](https://typenetwork.com/articles/popaganda-interview-giveaway) typeface as an expanded family represents a different stance. In an interview with Lucas Czarnecki, Brody noted that this typeface, drawing from reggae record labels and the agitprop tradition, was designed as an intentional tool of friction against today's notion of flawless typography. This attitude aligns with the argument formulated by Agyei Archer in his recent [article](https://blog.agyei.design/why-you-should-release-that-shitty/). Archer emphasized that the backlash against the sterile aesthetic created by AI and corporate technology is driving demand for imperfect yet characterful typefaces.

## Digital Infrastructure: CSS Conventions, Security, and Tools

Alex Kladov highlighted the *text-wrap: pretty* property, [explaining](https://webkit.org/blog/16547/better-typography-with-text-wrap-pretty/) ways to preserve paragraph integrity and achieve more balanced line breaks in web typography. This approach aims to reduce single-word final lines and produce more balanced text blocks by re-evaluating the closing portion of a paragraph. Meanwhile, the [tool](https://clagnut.com/sandbox/font-size-adjust.html) developed by Richard Rutter helps resolve visual inconsistencies arising from metric differences between fonts—such as x-height—using the *font-size-adjust* property.

Concurrently with these technical developments, the dimension of security was also brought to the fore. Paul Wood [analyzed](https://paultendo.github.io/posts/confusable-vision-visual-similarity/) 1,418 character pairs from Unicode's list of confusable characters at the pixel level across 230 system fonts. Finding that 96.5 percent of the pairs posed no visual threat, Wood determined that 82 pairs overlapped identically with Latin letters in at least one font. In this respect, the study demonstrates that confusability from a typographic perspective is not merely a character trait, and that typeface choice serves as a decisive factor, turning font selection directly into a security decision.

Software developer McCloskey [explained](https://mccloskeybr.com/articles/font_rendering.html) the computational pipeline extending from font files to pixel generation. Detailing why the *Signed Distance Fields* technique yields sharper results at every size and resolution, McCloskey demonstrates within a technical framework that typographic success in digital media depends not only on aesthetic choices, but also on the mathematical interpretation capabilities of display technologies.

This technical reality imposed by screen technologies intersects with the search for organic forms and the material production challenges faced by designers translating traditional practices into the digital sphere. Type designer John Roshell [recounted](https://adobe.design/stories/community/making-type-john-roshell-of-comicraft-swell-type) the process of bringing comic book lettering into the digital age. He emphasized having to design typefaces from scratch to bring computer-idealized geometric forms closer to the organic structure of handwritten characters, and that the time pressure of that era shaped both his productivity and expertise. Meanwhile, a [short documentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AGihdIyEHZI) produced about [House Industries](https://houseindustries.com/) founder Andy Cruz centers on the financial realities of the studio's founding period. Cruz shared that he had to sell his Porsche 944 Turbo to fund technical equipment, illustrating the economic reality of design practice through a personal example.
